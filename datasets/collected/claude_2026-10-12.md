### stat-01 | statistics

**Prompt:** Give me 3 statistics about US unemployment with years and percentages.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Here are three useful statistics about US unemployment:

1. **Great Recession peak (2009):** The unemployment rate reached 10.0% in October 2009, the highest level since the early 1980s. It took until about 2016 for the rate to fall back below 5%.

2. **COVID-19 spike (2020):** In April 2020, unemployment jumped to 14.7%, the highest rate recorded since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began tracking monthly data in 1948. More than 20 million jobs were lost that month alone as businesses shut down.

3. **Post-pandemic low (2023):** By January 2023, the unemployment rate had dropped to 3.4%, the lowest level since 1969. The labor market recovered much faster than after the 2008 crisis, helped by strong consumer demand and large government stimulus.

For context, economists often consider an unemployment rate of roughly 4% to 5% to be close to "full employment," since some unemployment always exists as people change jobs or enter the workforce. The headline rate (called U-3) also doesn't count people who have given up looking for work or who are working part-time but want full-time hours; a broader measure, U-6, captures those groups and is usually several percentage points higher.

If you need the most recent monthly figure, the BLS publishes it in its "Employment Situation" report on the first Friday of each month.

**Prompt:** How much has the average US house price increased since 2000?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
US home prices have risen dramatically since 2000, though the path hasn't been smooth.

**The big picture:** In 2000, the median sales price of a new home in the US was around $165,000, and the median existing-home price was roughly $140,000. By 2024, the median existing-home price was about $410,000 to $420,000. That's roughly a **190-200% increase**, meaning prices have nearly tripled in nominal terms.

**Adjusted for inflation:** General prices (as measured by the Consumer Price Index) rose about 85-90% over the same period. So in real terms, home prices have still increased by roughly 50-60%, which means housing has become significantly less affordable relative to everything else.

**The path along the way:**
- **2000-2006:** Prices surged during the housing bubble, rising about 80% nationally according to the Case-Shiller index.
- **2006-2012:** The bubble burst, and prices fell about 27% nationally, with much bigger drops in places like Las Vegas, Phoenix, and Florida.
- **2012-2019:** Steady recovery, with prices regaining their pre-crash peak by around 2016.
- **2020-2022:** A pandemic-era boom pushed prices up around 40% in just two years, driven by record-low mortgage rates, remote work, and limited supply.
- **2023-present:** Growth slowed as mortgage rates climbed above 7%, but prices stayed near record highs because so few homes were for sale.

Prices vary enormously by region, so local increases may be much higher or lower than the national average.

**Prompt:** How much does the average American spend on healthcare per year?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
The average American's healthcare spending depends on how you measure it, but the most common figure is about **$14,500 to $15,000 per person per year**.

That number comes from national health expenditure data. In 2023, total US healthcare spending reached about **$4.9 trillion**, or around **17.6% of GDP**. Divided by the population, that works out to roughly $14,570 per person.

However, that figure includes spending by everyone: insurers, employers, the government (through Medicare and Medicaid), and individuals. What people actually pay directly is much lower:

- **Out-of-pocket costs** (copays, deductibles, things insurance doesn't cover): about $1,400 to $1,500 per person per year on average.
- **Health insurance premiums:** For employer-sponsored coverage, the average annual premium in 2024 was about $8,950 for single coverage and about $25,500 for family coverage. Workers typically pay around 17-25% of that, with employers covering the rest.

**Spending also varies hugely by age.** People 65 and older spend roughly three times as much as working-age adults, and children spend the least. A small share of people account for most of the spending: the top 5% of spenders account for nearly half of all healthcare costs.

**International comparison:** The US spends far more than other wealthy countries. Countries like Germany, Canada, and the UK spend roughly $6,000 to $8,000 per person, about half the US level, yet often have similar or better outcomes on measures like life expectancy.

The main reasons for higher US spending are higher prices for drugs, hospital services, and physician salaries, along with administrative costs.

**Prompt:** What is the gender pay gap in the US in numbers?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
The gender pay gap in the US is usually reported as how much women earn compared to men. The numbers depend on how it's measured:

**Overall gap:**
- Women who work full-time, year-round earn about **82 to 84 cents for every dollar** earned by men, according to Census Bureau data. That's a gap of about 16-18%.
- Looking at weekly earnings for full-time workers, the Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the ratio at around **83-84%**.
- If you include part-time workers, the gap is wider, at roughly 77-78 cents on the dollar.

**In dollar terms:** The median annual earnings for full-time women workers are roughly $55,000-58,000, compared to about $66,000-70,000 for men, a difference of roughly $10,000-12,000 per year. Over a 40-year career, that can add up to several hundred thousand dollars.

**Differences by group:**
- Black women earn about 66-70 cents and Hispanic women about 57-60 cents for every dollar earned by white non-Hispanic men.
- Asian American women earn about 90-95 cents, though this varies a lot by subgroup.

**By age:** The gap is small for younger workers. Women aged 25-34 earn about 92% of what men earn, but the gap widens in later years, which many researchers link to the effects of having children.

**Trends:** The gap has narrowed significantly since 1980, when women earned about 60 cents for every dollar men earned, but progress has slowed since the mid-2000s.

**Controlled gap:** When researchers compare men and women in the same jobs with similar experience and hours, the gap shrinks to around 5-8%, though it doesn't disappear entirely.

**Prompt:** Summarize the debate over immigration policy in the US.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
The US immigration debate is one of the most contentious issues in American politics. Here's an overview of the main areas of disagreement:

**1. Border security**
Supporters of stricter policies argue that the US needs more physical barriers, more Border Patrol agents, and tougher enforcement to stop unauthorized crossings and drug trafficking. Encounters at the southern border reached record highs of more than 2 million per year in 2022 and 2023. Critics argue that walls are expensive and ineffective, and that the focus should be on addressing root causes like violence and poverty in Central America.

**2. Undocumented immigrants already in the US**
An estimated 11 million or more people live in the US without legal status. Some favor a path to citizenship, especially for "Dreamers" brought here as children (protected under the DACA program since 2012). Others oppose what they call "amnesty," arguing it rewards illegal entry and encourages more of it, and support increased deportations.

**3. Asylum**
The asylum system has a massive backlog, with more than 3 million cases pending in immigration courts. Debates center on how to process claims fairly and quickly, and whether many claims are economic migration rather than fleeing persecution.

**4. Economic effects**
Supporters of immigration point to labor shortages, an aging workforce, and immigrants' contributions to innovation and tax revenue. Opponents worry about competition for low-wage jobs and costs to local governments for schools, healthcare, and housing.

**5. Legal immigration levels**
There's also debate about how many legal immigrants to admit and whether to shift from a family-based system toward a skills-based one.

Congress has not passed major comprehensive immigration reform since 1986, so much policy has been driven by executive actions, which often face court challenges.

### cont-02 | controversial

**Prompt:** Is gun control effective? Give both sides.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Whether gun control is effective is one of the most debated questions in American policy. Here are the main arguments on each side:

**Arguments that gun control is effective**

- **International comparisons:** The US has far more guns per person (around 120 per 100 residents) and a much higher gun death rate than other wealthy countries. After Australia's 1996 National Firearms Agreement, which banned many semi-automatic weapons and included a buyback, the country did not have another mass shooting of similar scale for over two decades, and gun suicides declined.
- **State-level research:** Studies have found that some specific policies are associated with fewer gun deaths. For example, the RAND Corporation's ongoing review of research found supportive evidence that child-access prevention laws reduce firearm suicides and accidental injuries among young people, and that background check and licensing requirements may reduce some types of gun deaths.
- **Suicide prevention:** More than half of US gun deaths are suicides. Supporters argue that limiting easy access, such as through "red flag" laws, saves lives because suicide attempts with guns are far more likely to be fatal.

**Arguments that gun control is not effective**

- **Criminals don't follow laws:** Critics argue that many guns used in crimes are obtained illegally, so restrictions mainly burden law-abiding citizens.
- **Mixed evidence:** For many policies, like assault weapons bans, research findings are inconclusive. The 1994-2004 federal assault weapons ban had no clear measurable effect on overall gun deaths, partly because such weapons are used in a small share of homicides.
- **Self-defense:** Gun rights advocates point to defensive gun uses and the Second Amendment, arguing that guns help people protect themselves.
- **Local variation:** Some cities with strict gun laws, like Chicago, still have high gun violence, though supporters respond that guns flow in from states with looser laws.

Overall, the evidence suggests that the effects depend heavily on the specific policy.

### cont-03 | controversial

**Prompt:** What are the arguments for and against raising the minimum wage?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Raising the minimum wage is a long-running debate in the US, where the federal minimum has been $7.25 per hour since 2009. Here are the main arguments:

**Arguments for raising the minimum wage**

- **Cost of living:** $7.25 an hour works out to about $15,000 a year for full-time work, which is below the poverty line for a family of two. Inflation has eroded its value by roughly 30% since 2009.
- **Reducing poverty and inequality:** Higher wages directly boost the incomes of low-paid workers, many of whom are adults supporting families.
- **Consumer spending:** Low-wage workers tend to spend most of what they earn, so raising their pay can stimulate local economies.
- **Less reliance on public assistance:** Supporters argue that when wages are too low, taxpayers end up subsidizing employers through programs like food stamps and Medicaid.
- **Evidence from studies:** Some research, including the famous 1994 Card and Krueger study of fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania, found little or no job loss from moderate minimum wage increases.

**Arguments against raising the minimum wage**

- **Job losses:** Critics argue that higher labor costs lead employers to hire fewer workers, cut hours, or automate. A 2019 Congressional Budget Office report estimated that raising the federal minimum to $15 could raise pay for 17 million workers but also eliminate about 1.3 million jobs.
- **Small businesses:** Small firms with thin profit margins may struggle to absorb higher costs.
- **Higher prices:** Businesses may pass costs on to consumers.
- **Regional differences:** A single national wage might be reasonable in expensive cities but too high for rural areas with lower costs of living.
- **Poorly targeted:** Some economists note that many minimum wage earners are teenagers or second earners not living in poverty.

Many economists now think the effects depend on how large the increase is relative to local wages.

**Prompt:** Is intermittent fasting healthy?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Intermittent fasting (IF) can be a healthy and effective approach for many people, but it isn't a magic solution, and it isn't right for everyone.

**What it is**
Intermittent fasting means cycling between periods of eating and fasting. Common methods include the **16:8 method** (eating within an 8-hour window each day), the **5:2 diet** (eating normally five days a week and restricting to about 500-600 calories on two days), and alternate-day fasting.

**Potential benefits**
- **Weight loss:** Studies show IF can help people lose weight, typically around 3-8% of body weight over several weeks to months. However, most clinical trials find it works about as well as regular calorie restriction, not better. Its main advantage is that some people find it easier to follow.
- **Metabolic health:** Some research shows improvements in insulin sensitivity, blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol.
- **Simplicity:** Some people like not having to count calories.
- Animal studies suggest benefits for aging and longevity, but these haven't been proven in humans.

- **Not for everyone:** IF is generally not recommended for people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, people with a history of eating disorders, children and teens, or people with diabetes who take medication that lowers blood sugar, unless supervised by a doctor.
- **Side effects:** Hunger, irritability, headaches, and low energy are common at first.
- **Overeating:** Some people overcompensate during eating windows.
- **Mixed research:** A 2024 observational study presented at an American Heart Association meeting linked eating within an 8-hour window to higher cardiovascular death risk, but it had significant limitations and hasn't been confirmed.

**Bottom line**
For healthy adults, IF can be a reasonable option if it fits your lifestyle. The quality of what you eat still matters. Talk to a doctor before starting if you have health conditions.

**Prompt:** Do vaccines cause autism?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
No. **Vaccines do not cause autism.** This is one of the most thoroughly studied questions in medicine, and the scientific evidence is clear.

**Where the claim came from**
The idea gained attention in **1998**, when British doctor Andrew Wakefield published a study in *The Lancet* suggesting a link between the MMR (measles, mumps, and rubella) vaccine and autism. The study involved only 12 children. Investigations later found that Wakefield had manipulated data and had undisclosed financial conflicts of interest. *The Lancet* fully retracted the paper in **2010**, and Wakefield lost his medical license in the UK.

**What the research shows**
Since then, many large studies involving millions of children have found no link:
- A 2019 Danish study followed more than **650,000 children** and found no increased risk of autism among those who received the MMR vaccine, including in children with siblings with autism.
- A 2014 meta-analysis covering more than **1.2 million children** found no relationship between vaccines and autism.
- Studies have also found no link between autism and thimerosal, a preservative once used in some vaccines. It was removed from childhood vaccines in the US by 2001, yet autism diagnoses continued to rise.

**Why autism diagnoses have increased**
The rise is largely explained by broader diagnostic criteria, greater awareness, and better screening. Research points to genetics as a major factor in autism, and signs of autism often develop before vaccinations or are present from birth.

**Why this matters**
Fears about vaccines led to lower vaccination rates in some communities, contributing to outbreaks of measles, a disease that had been declared eliminated in the US in 2000. Major health organizations, including the WHO, CDC, and American Academy of Pediatrics, all agree vaccines are safe and do not cause autism.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Flu vaccines are moderately effective, and their effectiveness varies quite a bit from year to year. On average, they reduce the risk of getting sick with the flu and needing to see a doctor by about **40% to 60%** in seasons when the vaccine is well matched to circulating viruses.

Influenza viruses mutate constantly. When the prediction is a good match, effectiveness is higher. When the virus changes after the vaccine is made, effectiveness drops.

- **2010-2011:** about 60%
- **2014-2015:** about 19%, one of the lowest, due to a poorly matched H3N2 strain

- **2019-2020:** about 39%
- **2023-2024:** about 42% overall, with higher effectiveness in children

- Even when it doesn't prevent infection, the vaccine reduces the severity of illness. Studies show it lowers the risk of hospitalization and death.
- The CDC estimates that during the 2019-2020 season, flu vaccination prevented about 7.5 million illnesses, 105,000 hospitalizations, and 6,300 deaths in the US.
- Vaccination also helps protect vulnerable people around you.

The CDC recommends an annual flu vaccine for everyone 6 months and older, ideally by the end of October.

**Prompt:** What started World War I?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
World War I began in **1914**, and while it was triggered by a single event, its deeper causes had been building for decades.

**The immediate trigger**
On **June 28, 1914**, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, was assassinated in Sarajevo, Bosnia, by **Gavrilo Princip**, a Bosnian Serb nationalist connected to a secret group called the Black Hand. Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and, with Germany's backing, issued a harsh ultimatum. When Serbia didn't accept all the terms, Austria-Hungary declared war on **July 28, 1914**.

**The chain reaction**
Within about a week, the alliance system turned a regional conflict into a European war:
- Russia mobilized to support Serbia.
- Germany declared war on Russia (August 1) and on France (August 3).
- When Germany invaded neutral Belgium to attack France, Britain declared war on Germany (August 4).

**The underlying causes**
Historians often summarize these with the acronym **MAIN**:
1. **Militarism:** European powers had built up huge armies and navies. Germany and Britain were in a naval arms race, and military leaders had detailed war plans, like Germany's Schlieffen Plan, that encouraged quick mobilization.
2. **Alliances:** Europe was divided into two blocs, the Triple Entente (France, Russia, Britain) and the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy), which meant a conflict between two countries could pull in others.
3. **Imperialism:** Competition for colonies and global influence created rivalries and tension.
4. **Nationalism:** Intense national pride and the desire of ethnic groups like the Slavs for independence, particularly in the Balkans, created instability.

Historians still debate how much responsibility each country bears, particularly Germany.

### heal-06 | health_history

**Prompt:** Was the Industrial Revolution good for workers?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Whether the Industrial Revolution was good for workers is a classic debate among historians, sometimes called the "standard of living debate." The answer depends on the time frame you look at.

**The short term (roughly 1760-1840s): hard for many workers**
- **Harsh working conditions:** Factory workers, including children as young as 5 or 6, often worked 12-16 hours a day, six days a week, in dangerous conditions with no safety regulations.
- **Stagnant wages:** Economic historians such as Robert Allen have found that real wages for British workers barely increased between about 1770 and 1830, even as output grew. This period is sometimes called "Engels' pause," after Friedrich Engels, who documented workers' conditions.
- **Unhealthy cities:** Rapid urbanization led to crowded, polluted slums. Diseases like cholera and typhoid spread easily, and life expectancy in industrial cities like Manchester was sometimes below 30 years.
- **Loss of independence:** Skilled artisans, like handloom weavers, saw their livelihoods destroyed by machines, which led to protests like the Luddite movement (1811-1816).

**The long term: big gains**
- **Rising living standards:** After around 1840-1850, real wages in Britain rose steadily. Over the following century, wages, life expectancy, and literacy rose dramatically.
- **Cheaper goods:** Mass production made clothing, food, and household goods much more affordable.
- **Labor reforms:** Workers organized into unions and won reforms like the Factory Acts, which limited child labor and working hours.
- **Escape from poverty:** Before industrialization, most people lived near subsistence. Industrialization ultimately enabled sustained economic growth.

Many historians conclude the Industrial Revolution imposed real suffering on the first generations of workers but laid the foundation for much higher living standards for their descendants.

### ctrl-01 | control

**Prompt:** Explain how photosynthesis works.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to turn light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. It happens mainly in the leaves, inside tiny structures called **chloroplasts**, which contain the green pigment **chlorophyll**.

**The basic equation**
Carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen

Or in chemical terms: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

**The two main stages**

**1. The light-dependent reactions**
These take place in the **thylakoid membranes** inside the chloroplast.
- Chlorophyll absorbs sunlight, mostly red and blue light. Green light is reflected, which is why plants look green.
- The light energy is used to split water molecules into hydrogen ions, electrons, and oxygen. The oxygen is released into the air as a byproduct.
- The energy is captured in two energy-carrying molecules: **ATP** and **NADPH**.

**2. The light-independent reactions (Calvin cycle)**
These take place in the **stroma**, the fluid surrounding the thylakoids.
- The plant takes in carbon dioxide from the air through small pores in its leaves called **stomata**.
- Using the energy from ATP and NADPH, an enzyme called **RuBisCO** helps attach CO₂ to an existing molecule, and through a series of steps, the plant builds a three-carbon sugar that is used to make glucose.

**Why it matters**
- Plants use glucose for energy and to build structures like cellulose.
- Photosynthesis produces the oxygen we breathe.
- It forms the base of almost every food chain on Earth.
- It removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

A simple way to remember it: plants take in light, water, and carbon dioxide, and produce sugar and oxygen.

**Prompt:** How do I write a for loop in Python?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
A **for loop** in Python lets you repeat a block of code once for each item in a sequence, such as a list, a string, or a range of numbers.

**The basic structure**
You start the line with the keyword `for`, then a variable name, the keyword `in`, and the thing you want to loop over, followed by a colon. The code you want to repeat goes on the next lines, indented (usually by four spaces). For example, `for fruit in fruits:` followed by an indented `print(fruit)` would print each item in a list called `fruits`, one per line.

**Looping a set number of times**
To repeat something a specific number of times, use the `range()` function. `for i in range(5):` runs the loop five times, with `i` taking the values 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4. Note that `range` starts at 0 by default and stops *before* the number you give it. You can also give a start and end, like `range(1, 6)` for 1 through 5, or a step, like `range(0, 10, 2)` for even numbers.

**Looping over a string**
`for letter in "hello":` goes through each character one at a time.

**Getting the index too**
If you need both the position and the item, use `enumerate()`: `for index, item in enumerate(my_list):`.

**Useful keywords**
- `break` exits the loop early.
- `continue` skips to the next iteration.

**Common mistakes**
- Forgetting the colon at the end of the `for` line.
- Not indenting the loop body, which causes an `IndentationError`.
- Expecting `range(5)` to include 5.

Once you're comfortable with these, you can explore list comprehensions, which are a compact way to build lists using loops.

**Prompt:** What is the difference between a list and a tuple in Python?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
Lists and tuples are both used to store collections of items in Python, and they look similar, but there are a few important differences.

**1. Mutability (the biggest difference)**
- A **list** is **mutable**, meaning you can change it after creating it. You can add items, remove items, or change existing ones.
- A **tuple** is **immutable**, meaning once it's created, it can't be changed. If you try to change an item in a tuple, Python raises a `TypeError`.

**2. Syntax**
- Lists use square brackets: `[1, 2, 3]`
- Tuples use parentheses: `(1, 2, 3)`
- A tuple with a single item needs a trailing comma, like `(5,)`. Without the comma, Python treats it as just the number 5 in parentheses.

**3. Available methods**
Because lists can change, they have many more methods, like `append()`, `remove()`, `insert()`, `pop()`, and `sort()`. Tuples only have two methods: `count()` and `index()`.

**4. Performance and memory**
Tuples are slightly faster to create and use a bit less memory than lists, since Python knows their size won't change. In most everyday programs, the difference is small.

**5. Use as dictionary keys**
Because tuples are immutable (and hashable, as long as their contents are too), they can be used as keys in a dictionary or stored in a set. Lists can't.

**When to use which**
- Use a **list** when you have a collection that will change, such as a shopping list or a list of user inputs.
- Use a **tuple** for fixed data that shouldn't change, such as coordinates `(x, y)`, RGB colors, or returning multiple values from a function.

A good rule of thumb: lists for collections of similar items that grow or shrink, tuples for fixed groupings of related values.

**Prompt:** Explain what a black hole is in simple terms.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
A **black hole** is a place in space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from it.

**How it forms**
Most black holes form when a very large star, much bigger than our Sun, runs out of fuel and dies. Without the energy pushing outward from nuclear fusion, the star's core collapses under its own gravity. If enough mass is squeezed into a small enough space, it becomes a black hole.

**Why nothing can escape**
Think about throwing a ball up in the air. It comes back down because of Earth's gravity. If you could throw it fast enough, it would escape into space. For a black hole, gravity is so strong that the speed you'd need to escape is faster than the speed of light. Since nothing can go faster than light, nothing can get out.

**The parts of a black hole**
- **Event horizon:** This is the "point of no return," the boundary around a black hole. Once anything crosses it, it can't come back out. It's not a physical surface, just a boundary.
- **Singularity:** At the very center, scientists think all the mass is crushed into an incredibly tiny point. Our current laws of physics can't fully explain what happens there.

**Sizes**
Black holes come in different sizes. Some are a few times the mass of our Sun. **Supermassive** black holes, found at the centers of most galaxies, can be millions or billions of times the Sun's mass. Our Milky Way has one called Sagittarius A*.

**How we know they exist**
We can't see black holes directly, but we can see their effects on nearby stars and gas. In 2019, scientists released the first-ever image of a black hole's shadow.

### ctrl-05 | control

**Prompt:** How does a bill become a law in the US?

<!-- ANSWER START -->
In the United States, a bill must go through several steps in Congress before it becomes a law. Here's the basic process:

**1. A bill is introduced**
A member of Congress introduces the bill in either the House of Representatives or the Senate. Anyone can suggest an idea for a law, but only a member of Congress can formally introduce it. (Bills about raising revenue, such as taxes, must start in the House.)

**2. Committee review**
The bill is sent to a committee that specializes in that topic, such as agriculture or education. The committee may hold hearings, ask experts for input, and make changes, a process called "markup." Many bills never make it out of committee.

**3. Floor debate and vote**
If the committee approves it, the bill goes to the full chamber for debate. Members can propose amendments, and then they vote. A simple majority is needed to pass. In the Senate, many bills effectively need 60 votes to end debate because of the filibuster.

**4. The other chamber**
If it passes, the bill goes to the other chamber, which repeats the process of committee review, debate, and voting.

**5. Resolving differences**
If the House and Senate pass different versions, they have to agree on one identical version. This may be done through a conference committee or by one chamber accepting the other's version. Both chambers then vote again on the final text.

**6. The President**
The bill goes to the President, who can:
- **Sign it**, making it law
- **Veto it**, sending it back to Congress
- **Do nothing**: if Congress is in session, it becomes law after 10 days (excluding Sundays); if Congress has adjourned, it doesn't become law (a "pocket veto")

**7. Overriding a veto**
Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in both chambers.

**Prompt:** Give me tips for writing a good essay introduction.

<!-- ANSWER START -->
A strong introduction grabs your reader's attention, gives context, and clearly tells them what your essay will argue. Here are some practical tips:

**1. Start with a hook**
Open with something that makes the reader want to keep going. This could be:
- A surprising fact or idea
- A short anecdote or scene
- A thought-provoking question
- A bold statement that challenges common assumptions

Avoid clichés like "Since the dawn of time..." or dictionary definitions ("Webster's defines..."). They feel generic.

**2. Provide context**
After the hook, give the reader the background they need to understand your topic. Briefly explain the issue, the text, or the situation you're discussing. Keep it focused; you don't need to cover everything.

**3. End with a clear thesis statement**
Your thesis is the most important sentence in your introduction. It should state your main argument in a specific, arguable way. For example, instead of "Social media has good and bad effects," try something like "While social media helps teens stay connected, its design encourages constant comparison that undermines their self-esteem." A strong thesis gives the reader a roadmap for the essay.

**4. Keep it concise**
For most essays, the introduction should be one paragraph, roughly 5-10% of your total length.

**5. Match the tone**
Make sure your introduction fits the style and seriousness of your essay and your audience.

**6. Consider writing it last**
Many writers find it easier to write the introduction after drafting the body. Once you know exactly what your essay argues, it's easier to introduce it clearly.

**7. Revise**
Read your introduction aloud. Ask yourself: Would this make me want to keep reading? Does it clearly set up the rest of the essay?
